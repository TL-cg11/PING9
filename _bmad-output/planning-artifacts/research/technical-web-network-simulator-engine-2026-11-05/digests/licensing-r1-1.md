### Q3. Trademarks

8. Cisco's public trademark list does not include "Packet Tracer" or "Cisco Packet Tracer". It does list "IOS®", "Cisco IOS®", "CCNA®", "Networking Academy®" and "Cisco Networking Academy®" as registered. | https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/about/legal/trademarks.html | Cisco Systems | undated | accessed 2026-11-05 | high (as reported by the fetch tool's reading of the page) | trademark
9. Absence from the list is not evidence that Cisco makes no claim: both the list page and Cisco's trademark policy say the listing "is not all-inclusive" and that absence of a mark "does not mean that it is not a Cisco trademark". Cisco consistently brands the product "Cisco Packet Tracer" (finding 5). | https://www.cisco.com/c/dam/en_us/about/ac50/ac47/downloads/logo/trademark.pdf | Cisco Systems | May 2009 (footer "© 2000-2009 … DH05/09") | accessed 2026-11-05 | high | trademark
10. Cisco's Policy on Trademarks says: "Do not use any Cisco trademark or name as or as part of a company, product, service, solution, technology, or program name"; do not use any designation "confusingly similar" to a Cisco trademark; "Do not register or use any domain name that incorporates any Cisco mark or name"; and do not combine or hyphenate a Cisco mark with other words. | same URL | Cisco Systems | May 2009 | accessed 2026-11-05 | high for the text; medium for currency (16-year-old PDF still served by cisco.com; a newer Brand Center policy may exist and was not read) | trademark
11. The same policy also says not to "copy or imitate any Cisco trade dress, type style, logo, product packaging, or the look, design, or overall commercial impression" of Cisco websites or materials, and not to use any Cisco logo without written permission. | same URL | Cisco Systems | May 2009 | accessed 2026-11-05 | high for the text | trademark
12. The policy allows referential use: Cisco "acknowledges that the use of Cisco trademarks, excluding any Cisco logos, may be necessary to refer to Cisco's products" or to describe subject matter; such use must be "accurate and descriptive", must not be the visual focal point or more prominent than your own name, must not imply affiliation, and should carry the ownership acknowledgement. Permission requests go through Cisco's online request tool. | same URL | Cisco Systems | May 2009 | accessed 2026-11-05 | high for the text | trademark
13. Synthesis (mine): this is Cisco's unilateral policy, not a statement of law, and the registration status of "Packet Tracer" was not verified. But under the policy's own terms, a product named with the words "Packet Tracer" is what Cisco says it does not permit if Cisco treats the phrase as its mark, and "IOS" is confirmed as a registered Cisco mark (finding 8), so "IOS-like CLI" should be descriptive wording only, never part of the product name. Whether "Packet Tracer" is legally protectable or registered in the US or Korea needs a registry check and, for anything public-facing, a lawyer's judgement. | — | — | — | — | medium | trademark

### Q6. FRRouting

24. FRR's top-level COPYING file says the project "consists of parts with various licenses", each file marked by SPDX identifier, with full texts in doc/licenses. The compiled composite work "is thought to always be distributable under GPLv2 or later", which the file calls the community's "best-effort understanding" and "not a legal statement". Documentation is under a separate permissive licence inherited from GNU Zebra. | https://github.com/FRRouting/frr/blob/master/COPYING | FRRouting project | undated (master branch) | accessed 2026-11-05 | high | licensing
25. The FSF's GPL FAQ says the GPL "permits anyone to make a modified version and use it without ever distributing it to others", and that a company running a modified GPL program on a web site "does not have to release the modified sources"; the AGPL is the licence that adds a network-use source requirement. By extension, running unmodified FRR server-side only is not distribution under this reading. | https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html | Free Software Foundation | undated | accessed 2026-11-05 (first 100,000 of ~156,000 characters read) | high for the text; it is FSF's interpretation, not a court ruling | licensing
26. Same FAQ: "if you distribute binaries, you must distribute the complete corresponding source code too"; if object code is offered on a network server, the source must be offered on a network server as well; transferring copies to other organisations or individuals is distribution. | same URL | Free Software Foundation | undated | accessed 2026-11-05 | high for the text | licensing
27. Synthesis (mine): the obligation switches on the moment a copy leaves the team's control. FRR (or VyOS) running in containers on the team's server with students connecting through a browser terminal → no source-distribution duty under the FAQ's reading. Handing students a VM/container image, an installer, or a browser-delivered build (e.g. compiled to WebAssembly and downloaded to the client) → that is conveying binaries, and licence text plus corresponding source (or a valid offer) must accompany it. The WebAssembly case is my inference; no source read addresses it directly. | — | — | — | — | medium | licensing
